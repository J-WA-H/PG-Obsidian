When promoting a calculation from leading order to next-to-leading order, the method of calculating cross-sections for an $n$-body final state in hadronic collisions, [(7.1)](Hadronic%20Cross-Sections), will be altered. This is due to the fact that the partonic cross-section, $\hat{\sigma}$ now receives contributions not only from the Born-level matrix elements but also from virtual and real corrections. Therefore at NLO
$$\sigma^{(NLO)}=\sum_{a,b}\int_{0}^{1}dx_{a}dx_{b}f_{a/h_{1}}(x_{a},\mu_{F})f_{b/h_{2}}(x_{b},\mu_{F})\int d\hat{\sigma}_{ab\to n}^{(NLO)}(\mu_{F},\mu_{R})=\int d\Phi_{\mathcal{B}}[\mathcal{B}_{n}(\phi_{\mathcal{B}};\mu_{F},\mu_{R})+\mathcal{V}_{n}(\Phi_{\mathcal{B}};\mu_{F},\mu_{R})]+\int d\Phi_{\mathcal{R}}\mathcal{R}_{n}(\Phi_{\mathcal{R}};\mu_{F},\mu_{R}),\tag{1.1}$$
where the various contributions - Born term $\mathcal{B}$, virtual correction term $\mathcal{V}$, and real correction term $\mathcal{R}$ - are given by suitably helicity-summed or averaged matrix elements. Denoting the perturbative order of the Born-level contribution with $b$, and indicating the orders of the matrix element $\mathcal{M}^{(b)}$ accordingly, therefore
$$\mathcal{B}_{n}(\Phi_{\mathcal{B}};\mu_{F},\mu_{R})=\overline{\sum_{h}}|\mathcal{M}^{(b)}_{n}(\Phi_{\mathcal{B}},h;\mu_{F},\mu_{R})|^2 \\ \mathcal{V}_{n}(\Phi_{\mathcal{B}};\mu_{F},\mu_{R})=2 \overline{\sum_{h}}\mathrm{Re}[\mathcal{M}_{n}^{(b)}(\Phi_{\mathcal{B}},h;\mu_{F},\mu_{R})\mathcal{M}^{*(b+1)}_{n}(\Phi_{\mathcal{B}},h;\mu_{F},\mu_{R})] \\ \mathcal{R}_{n}(\Phi_{\mathcal{R}};\mu_{F},\mu_{R})=2\overline{\sum_{h}}|\mathcal{M}_{n+1}^{(b+1)}(\Phi_{\mathcal{R}},h;\mu_{F},\mu_{R})|^{2}\tag{1.2}.$$
At this point it is important to stress that the Born-level and virtual matrix elements are multiplied to yield the overall virtual correction. Thereby, the latter matrix element that emerges from the former by adding a closed loop without changing the external particles of the process $ab\to n$. Both matrix elements share the same phase space, essentially an $n$-body final-state phase space, $\Phi_{\mathcal{B}}$. In contrast, the real correction emerges as the square of matrix elements with one additional outgoing particle, this may lead to replacing an incoming particle type $a,b$ with $a',b'$. For instance, an incoming quark may be replaced by a gluon, which splits into an outgoing anti-quark and a quark $a$ such that for the process in question $ab\to n$ is replaced by $gb\to n+\bar{a}$. With this change also the PDFs must change accordingly.

Turning to the phase space elements, the expressions for the Born-level and real correction phase space are given by
$$d\Phi_{\mathcal{B}}=dx_{a}dx_{b}f_{a/h_{1}}(x_{a},\mu_{F})f_{b/h_{2}}(x_{b},\mu_{F}) \frac{1}{2\hat{s}_{ab}}d\Phi_{n} \\d \Phi_{\mathcal{R}}=dx_{a'}dx_{b'}f_{a'/h_{1}}(x_{a'},\mu_{F})f_{b'/h_{2}}(x_{b'},\mu_{F}) \frac{1}{2\hat{s}_{a'b'}}d\Phi_{n+1}, \tag{1.3}$$
with
$$d\Phi_{n}=\prod_{i=1}^{n} \frac{d^4p_{i}}{(2\pi)^{4}}(2\pi)\delta(p_{i}^{2}-m_{i}^{2})(2\pi)^{4}\delta^{(4)}\left( p_{a}+p_{b}-\sum_{i}p_{i} \right)\Theta(E_{i})\tag{1.4}.$$
There are complications when having to calculate higher order cross-sections beyond the increased number and more complicated diagrams. First of all, the loops in the virtual contribution introduce a new integration variable, the four-momentum in the loop $\ell$ which is not constrained by overall 4-momentum conservation or on-shell conditions encoded in the $\delta$-functions, but instead can take any value in any of its components allowed by 4-momentum conservation in the interaction vertices. In particular, the 4-momentum in the loop can become infinitely large; for expressions of the general form $d^4\ell/\ell^{m}$ this will potentially yield infinite results if the power of the loop momentum $m\leq 4$. Such ultraviolet divergences have to be regularised, which usually is achieved by employing dimensional regularisation, corresponding to integrating in $D=1-2\varepsilon$ rather than in 4 dimensions. The benefit of this method is that it can be formulated in such a way that Lorentz and gauge invariance are guaranteed which is not the case in other regularisation methods through a cut-off. In dimensional regularisation the UV divergences manifest themselves as simple poles $1/\varepsilon$. Having thus quantified the degree of divergence the theory is renormalised through a suitable, scheme dependent redefinition of the Lagrangian, adding suitable counterterms.

In addition to the UV divergences in the virtual corrections, IR divergences also occur in both the virtual and real corrections. In both cases they are related to emissions, either in the loop in the virtual correction or in the additional particle radiated in the real correction, where the additional particle has zero energy or is parallel to another particle. These divergences need to cancel each other in physically meaningful observables.

Again, in order to deal with IR divergences they have to be regularised, with dimensional regularisation as the method of choice. This time, the divergences manifest themselves as either double or single poles, $1/\varepsilon^2,1/\varepsilon$ respectively. As already implied, these poles do not need to be renormalised since they will cancel in the total result.

There is a problem with cancellation, as these poles show up in two parts of the cancellation, and in two in principle independent integrations: for the infrared divergences in the virtual contribution, they appear in the integral over the $n$-body phase space associated with Born configurations, while for their counterpart in the real contribution, they appear in the $(n+1)$-body final state. Even if these integrations could be performed analytically, they are cumbersome to trace in $D$-dimensions. More generally the phase space integration can be performed using Monte Carlo methods, but in this case it is impossible to integrate in $D$ dimensions, and other methods have to be found to isolate the divergences.
