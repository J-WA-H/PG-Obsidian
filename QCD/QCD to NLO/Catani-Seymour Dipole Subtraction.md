Subtraction methods based on matrix elements are not particularly useful when considering automation of subtraction methods as there are a large number of matrix elements that are being used. Therefore an algorithm is needed to implement subtraction methods.

The Catani-Seymour dipole subtraction method is to treat the emission of additional particles in the phase space in such a way that the soft/collinear limits of the individual emission factorises from underlying Born-type configurations. This is possible, in a process-independent way, by realising that in the soft limit the emission cross-section of an additional particle - typically a gluon or a photon - can be written as a product of an eikonal, and a corresponding Born level cross-section. Similarly in the collinear limit, emissions can be written as the product of a Born-level cross-section with a splitting kernal. In Catani-Seymour subtraction these two limits, soft and collinear, of the emission are analysed by introducing a spectator parton $k$. The spectator parton also allows the construction of phase-space mappings $i+j+k\to \{ ij \}+k$ for the combination of partons $i,j$ into the emitter $\{ ij \}$ with momentum $\tilde{p}_{ij}$, while parton $k$ accounts for the recoil and changes its momentum from $p_{k}$ to $\tilde{p}_{k}$. This allows all particles to be kept on their mass shell at all times but it also directly leads to a factorisation of the matrix elements and the phase space. The latter fact is important to allow a calculation of Born-level matrix elements with one less particle. The catch in this construction now is that these extra emission bits, $\{ ij \}+k\to i+j+k$ can be integrated analytically in $D$ dimensions, thereby allowing them to be subtracted in differential form from the real-emission matrix element and added back to the virtual contribution in integrated form.

In order to put both types of contributions together, it is sensible to decompose the eikonal cross-section term $W(p_{1},p_{2};k)$ with its symmetry between both emitting partons, with light momenta $p_{1},p_{2}$ into two individual terms with a definite emitter and spectator assignment. Including colour factors, they schematically read
$$W(p_{1},p_{2};k)=-\frac{\vec{T}_{1}\cdot \vec{T}_{2}}{2}\left( \frac{p_{1}^{\mu}}{p_{1}k}-\frac{p_{2}^{\mu}}{p_{2}k} \right)^{2}=\vec{T}_{1}\cdot \vec{T}_{2} \frac{p_{1}p_{2}}{(p_{1}k)(p_{2}k)}=\vec{T}_{1}\cdot \vec{T}_{2}\left[ \frac{p_{1}p_{2}}{(p_{1}k)(p_{1}k+p_{2}k)}+\frac{p_{1}p_{2}}{(p_{2}k)(p_{1}k+p_{2}k)} \right]=\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{1k;2}+\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{2k;1},\tag{5.1}$$
where the two dipole terms $\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{ik;j}$ have been introduced, with $i$ denoting the emitter or splitter, $k$ being the emitted particle, and $j$ denoting the spectator. 

Analysing these terms shows that they both diverge in the soft and collinear limit. At the same time, the individual terms do not diverge for the emitted momentum $k$ going parallel to the spectator momentum. It also helps to analyse singularities related to the soft and soft collinear ones in colour space, which involve leading and sub leading colour contributions and the hard collinear divergences encoded in splitting functions and are a leading colour effect. This means the eikonal-based dipoles above have been generalised in such a way that they also contain the collinear bits encoded in suitable splitting kernals, Full dipole subtraction terms $\mathcal{D}$ are introduced that emerge from the combination of Born-level matrix elements squared, including all symmetry and PDF factors, with terms similar to $\tilde{\mathcal{D}}$. The catch here is that this factorisation of the full subtraction matrix element into Born-level parts and individual emission terms also includes a factorisation of the phase space such that for these terms
$$\Phi_{\mathcal{R}}=\Phi_{\mathcal{B}}\otimes \Phi_{1}\tag{5.2}.$$
Considering in the following a $2\to n$ process at Born-level with momenta $p_{a}p_{b}\to p_{1}p_{2}\dots p_{n}$, the subtraction procedure uses two terms: a differential term, which can be written in the form of a sum of differential dipole cross-sections with an additionally emitted parton $\mathcal{D}(p_{a},p_{b};p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{n+1})$ to be subtracted from the real-emission contribution,
$$\mathcal{S}(\Phi_{\mathcal{R}})=\sum_{\text{dipoles}}\mathcal{D}(p_{a},p_{b};p_{1},p_{2},\dots,p_{n+1})=\sum_{ij,k}\mathcal{B}_{ij;k}(\Phi_{\mathcal{B}})\otimes \tilde{D}_{ij,k}(\Phi_{1})\to \mathcal{B}(\Phi_{\mathcal{B}})\otimes \mathbf{D}(\Phi_{1})\tag{5.3}.$$
Here, to simplify the notation, the sum over the dipole terms $\tilde{\mathcal{D}}_{ij;k}(\Phi_{1})$ has been replaced with the product of a dipole operator $\mathbf{D}(\Phi_{1})$, with the summation implicit.

This structure is reflected by a sum of the corresponding integrated terms, to be added back to the virtual contribution,
$$\mathcal{I}^{(\mathcal{S})}(\Phi_{\mathcal{B}},\varepsilon)=\sum_{\text{dipoles}}\mathcal{I}^{(\mathcal{D})}(p_{a},p_{b};p_{1},p_{2},\dots p_{i=1},p_{i+1},\dots ,p_{n+1})=\sum_{ij,k}\mathcal{B}_{ij,k}(\Phi_{\mathcal{B}})\otimes \mathcal{I}_{ij;k}^{(\mathcal{D})}(\Phi_{\mathcal{B}}) \to \mathcal{B}(\Phi_{\mathcal{B}})\otimes  \mathbf{D}(\Phi_{\mathcal{B}}).\tag{5.4}$$
Here one final state has been integrated out. This stresses that there are only $n$ particles in the final state of this integrated terms, which therefore have Born-level kinematics.
