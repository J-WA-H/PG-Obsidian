When evaluating matrix elements we have do deal with spinors and polarisation vectors of the external states. This can become very tedious as there are massive matrix multiplications coming from the $\gamma$-matrices. An easier way is to decompose the amplitude into scalar products of spinors and of Lorentz structures.

One frequently used spinor representation is based on two-component spinors, also known as Weyl spinors, which form a basic representation of the Lorentz group. To see how this works, realise that the Lorentz group is generated by 6 infinitesimal boost and rotation transformations along and around $x,y,z$. With the commutators of these generators forming a nonintuitive algebra, they can be rearranged into a set of six linearly independent linear combinations which decompose into two $SU(2)$ algebras, meaning the Lorentz group is the product of two $SU(2)$ groups. These groups have their first non-trivial representation through Weyl spinors associated with left-handed and right-handed spinors. The components of these two kinds of Weyl spinors are indicated by dotted and undotted indices $\alpha$ and $\dot{\alpha}$, each of which ranges only from $1$ to $2$, $a,\dot{a}\in \{ 1,2 \}$. For instance, for a massless four-vector $k$,
$$\zeta_{a}(k)=\begin{bmatrix}
\sqrt{ k^{+} } \\
\sqrt{ k^{-} }e^{ i\phi_{k} }
\end{bmatrix}\tag{3.1}$$
where light-cone momenta $k^{\pm}=k^{0}\pm k^{3}$ and $\phi_{k}$ is the angle of the transverse momentum $\vec{k}_{\perp}$. By using the complex plane, $k_{\perp}=k_{1}+ik_{2}$ and $\phi_{k}=\mathrm{arg}(\phi_{k})$, there is a fair amount of freedom in the definition of the spinors, like the definition of the axis defining the light-cone momentum directions or the orientation used in the definition of the angle $\phi_{k}$. This freedom can then be used for checks of the actual calculation, which must be independent of these choices.

However, the scalar product of such spinors $\zeta_{a}(k)=\ket{k}$ and $\zeta_{\dot{a}}=[k]$ can be written as
$$\eta_{a}(k)\zeta^{a}(q)=\eta_{a}(k)\varepsilon^{ab}\zeta_{b}(q)=\langle kq \rangle \\ \eta_{\dot{a}}(k)\zeta^{\dot{a}}(q)=[kq]=\langle kq \rangle ^{*} \tag{3.2}$$
where the latter relation holds true because trivially one could identify $\eta_{\dot{a}}=\eta_{a}^{*}$ and where the fact that the individual spinor components must be Grassman numbers, anti-commuting, is encapsulated in the "spinor" metric $\varepsilon^{ab}$ given by
$$\varepsilon_{ab}=\varepsilon^{ab}=\varepsilon_{\dot{a}\dot{b}}=\varepsilon^{\dot{a}\dot{b}}=\begin{bmatrix}
0 & 1 \\
-1 & 0
\end{bmatrix}\tag{3.3}.$$
The scalar product uses the momentum to label the spinor and the bracket to denote whether the indices are dotted or undotted. This can be used to decompose massive Dirac fermion spinors into two Weyl spinors arranged in a bi-spinor. This however introduced a gauge like degree of freedom into their definition.

The polarisation vectors for external and internal gauge bosons also have to be dealt with. In general, four-vectors $k^{\mu}$ can also be represented by Weyl spinors, by realising that
$$k^{\mu}=\sigma^{\mu}_{\dot{a} b}\zeta^{\dot{a}}(k)\zeta^{b}(k)\tag{3.4}$$
where $\sigma$ are the Pauli matrices. By introducing a light-like gauge vector $q$, polarisation vectors for external massless particles with four-momentum $p^{\mu}$ can be written as
$$\varepsilon^{\mu}_{\pm}(p,q)= \pm \frac{1}{\sqrt{ 2 }} \frac{{\bra{q^{\mp}} \sigma^{\mu}}\ket{p^{\pm}}}{\langle q^{\mp}p^{\pm} \rangle }\tag{3.5}. $$
It is also interesting to note that regular scalar products of four-vectors can be rewritten in the form of spinor products, for instance
$$2k\cdot q=\langle kq \rangle [kq]\tag{3.6}.$$
This relation and others like it make this spinor representation a powerful to rewrite Feynman amplitudes in a form that lends itself to the automation of scattering amplitude calculations through numerical methods.
