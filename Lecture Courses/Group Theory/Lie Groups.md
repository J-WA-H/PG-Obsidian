Unlike [[Finite Groups]], suppose group elements $g\in G$ depend smoothly on a set of continuous parameters $g(\alpha)$.

Since the identity is an important element, it is useful to parameterise the elements (at least those close to the identity) in such a way that $\alpha=0$ corresponds to the identity element. Thus we assume that in some neighbourhood of the identity, the group elements can be described by a function $N$ of real parameters $\alpha_{a}$ for $a=1,\dots,N$ s.t.
$$g(\alpha)\lvert_{\alpha=0}=e \tag{2.1}.$$
Then if we find a representation of the group, the linear operators will be parameterised the same way
$$D(\alpha)\lvert_{\alpha=0}=\mathbb{1}.\tag{2.2}$$
Then in some neighbourhood around the identity element we can Taylor expand $D(\alpha)$, and if we are close enough, just keep the first term
$$D(d\alpha)=1+id\alpha_{a}X_{a}\tag{2.3},$$
where we have just called the parameter $d\alpha$ to emphasise it is infinitesimal and 
$$X_{a}=-i \left.\frac{\partial}{\partial\alpha_{a}}D(\alpha)\right|_{\alpha=0}.\tag{2.4}$$
The $X_{a}$ for $a=1,\dots,N$ are the generators of the group. If the parameterisation is parsimonious (all parameters are actually needed to distinguish different group elements), the $X_{a}$ will be independent. The $i$ is included so that if the representation is unitary, the generators are hermitian.

In any particular direction, the group multiplication law is uncomplicated. There is a one-parameter family of group elements of the form
$$U(\lambda)=e^{ i\lambda \alpha_{a}X_{a} }\tag{2.5},$$
and the group multiplication law $U(\lambda_{1})U(\lambda_{2})=U(\lambda_{1}\lambda_{2})$.

However, if we multiply group elements generated by two different combination of generators, this doesn't hold in general, $e^{ i\alpha_{a}X_{a} }e^{ i\beta_{b} X_{b}}\neq e^{ i(\alpha_{a}+\beta_{a})X_{a} }$. But, since the exponentials form a representation of the group, the product must also be the exponential of some generator,
$$e^{ i\alpha_{a}x_{a} }e^{ i\beta_{b}x_{b} }=e^{ i\delta_{a}X_{a} }\tag{2.6}.$$
Since everything is smooth, we rewrite this to
$$i\delta_{a}X_{a}=\ln(1+e^{ i\alpha_{a}X_{a} }e^{ i\beta_{b}X_{b} }-1)\tag{2.7},$$
which expanding gives
$$i\delta_{a}X_{a}=-\frac{1}{2}[\alpha_{a}X_{a},\beta_{b}X_{b}]+\dots \tag{2.8}$$
$$\implies [\alpha_{a}X_{a},\beta_{b}X_{b}]=-2i(\delta_{c}-\alpha_{c}-\beta_{c})X_{c}+\dots\equiv i\gamma_{c}X_{c}\tag{2.9}$$
Since this is true $\forall\;\alpha,\beta$, $\gamma_{c}=\alpha_{a}\beta_{b}f_{abc}$, where $f_{abc}$ are the structure constants, and summarise virtually the entire group multiplication law, and are defined by
$$[X_{a},X_{b}]=if_{abc}X_{c}\tag{2.10}.$$
