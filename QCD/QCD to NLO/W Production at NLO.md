To give an example of subtraction it is useful to look at the real radiation contributions NLO corrections to $W$ production, in particular the process $u\bar{d}\to gW^{+}$. The squared matrix element for this process is
$$|\mathcal{M}|^2_{u\bar{d}\to g W^{+}}= \frac{|\mathcal{M}^{(LO)}|^2_{u\bar{d}\to W^{+}}}{m_{W}^{2}}(4\pi\alpha_{s}C_{F}) \frac{{\hat{t}^2+\hat{u}^2+2m_{W}^2\hat{s}}}{\hat{t}\hat{u}}\tag{4.1}.$$
This is divergent in the limits $\hat{t},\hat{u}\to 0$. The key to the general approach for handling these IR singularities lies in the ability to deal with collinear singularities associated with each of these limits separately. This can be seen by analysing the kinematic dependence, isolating the divergent terms and then using partial fractioning
$$\frac{{\hat{t}^2+\hat{u}^2+2m_{W}^2\hat{s}}}{\hat{t}\hat{u}}=\frac{{(\hat{t}+\hat{u}^2)+2m_{W}^{2}\hat{s}}}{\hat{t}\hat{u}}-2=\left( \frac{1}{\hat{t}}+\frac{1}{\hat{u}} \right) {\frac{(\hat{t}+\hat{u})^2+2m_{W}^{2}\hat{s}}{\hat{t}+\hat{u}}}-2=\left( \frac{1}{\hat{t}}+\frac{1}{\hat{u}} \right)\left[ (m_{W}^{2}-\hat{s})+\frac{2m_{W}^{2}\hat{s}}{m_{W}^{2}-\hat{s}} \right]-2\tag{4.2}.$$
Overall momentum conservation has been employed, embodied by the identity $\hat{s}+\hat{t}+\hat{u}=m_{W}^{2}$. Furthermore the terms in the square brackets can be written in terms of the single dimensionless quantity $x=m_{W}^{2}/\hat{s}$ leading to
$$|\mathcal{M}^{(LO)}_{u\bar{d}\to g W^{+}}|^2=\frac{2\pi C_{F}\alpha_{s}(\mu_{R})}{m_{W}^2}|\mathcal{M}_{u\bar{d}\to W^{+}}^{(LO)}|^2 \left[ \frac{{\hat{t}^2+\hat{u}^2+2m_{W}^{2}\hat{s}}}{\hat{t}\hat{u}} \right] = \frac{2\pi C_{F}\alpha_{s}(\mu_{R})}{x}|\mathcal{M}^{(LO)}_{u\bar{d}\to W^{+}}|^2 \left[ \left( \frac{1}{\hat{t}}+\frac{1}{\hat{u}} \right)\left( -\frac{2}{1-x}+x+1 \right)-\frac{2x}{m_{W}^{2}} \right]\tag{4.3}$$
for the amplitude squared of the real emission contribution. Isolating the divergent terms $\propto 1/\hat{t},1/\hat{u}$, the equation above can be cast into the form
$$|\mathcal{M}_{u\bar{d}\to gW^{+}}^{(LO)}|^2=\frac{1}{x} |\mathcal{M}^{(LO)}_{u\bar{d}\to gW^{+}}|^2[\mathcal{D}(\hat{t},x)+\mathcal{D}(\hat{u},x)+\mathcal{R}(x)].\tag{4.4}$$
The infrared-singular terms are expressed in terms of the function $\mathcal{D}(\hat{t},x)$, where
$$\mathcal{D}(\hat{t},x)=8\pi\alpha_{s}C_{F}\left[ -\frac{1}{\hat{t}}\left( \frac{2}{1-x}-1-x \right) \right]\tag{4.5}$$
and the non-singular remainder reads
$$\mathcal{R}(x)=8\pi\alpha_{s}C_{F}\left[ -\frac{2x}{m_{W}^{2}} \right]\tag{4.6}.$$
The sum of the two singular terms forms the subtraction term,
$$\mathcal{S}(\Phi_{\mathcal{R}})=\frac{1}{x}|\mathcal{M}^{(LO)}_{u\bar{d}\to W^{+}}|^{2}[\mathcal{D}(\hat{t},x)+\mathcal{D}(\hat{u},x)]\tag{4.7}.$$
To compute the corresponding integrated subtraction term, $\mathcal{I}^{(\mathcal{S})}$, first the phase space must be appropriately factorised to allow an analytic integration over the one-particle phase space of the emitted gluon. The phase space is given by
$$d\Phi_{Wg}=\frac{d^Dp_{W}}{(2\pi)^{D}}(2\pi)\delta(p_{W}^2-m_{W}^2) \frac{d^Dp_{g}}{(2\pi)^{D}}(2\pi)\delta(p_{g}^{2})(2\pi)^{D}\delta^{(D)}(p_{a}+p_{b}-p_{W}-p_{g})=(2\pi)^{2-D} \frac{d^{D-1}p_{g}}{2E} \delta((p_{a}+p_{b}-P_{g})^2-m_{W}^2)\tag{4.8}$$
where $E$ represents the energy of the gluon. It is straightforward to evaluate this in the centre of mass frame,
$$d\Phi_{Wg}=\frac{(2\pi)^{2\varepsilon-2}}{2\sqrt{ \hat{s} }}\left( \frac{\hat{t}\hat{u}}{\hat{s}} \right)^{-\varepsilon}d\left( -\frac{{\hat{t}+\hat{u}}}{2\sqrt{ \hat{s} }} \right)d\hat{t}d\Omega^{1-2\varepsilon}\delta(\hat{s}+\hat{t}+\hat{u}-m_{W}^2)\tag{4.9}$$
where $d\Omega^{D-3}$ is the solid angle in $D-3$ dimensions,
$$\int d\Omega^{1-2\varepsilon}=\frac{2\pi}{\pi^{\varepsilon}\Gamma(1-\varepsilon)}\tag{4.10}.$$
It is convenient to use the two dimensionless variables $x$, $v=-\hat{t}/\hat{s}$. The phase-space integral can be expressed through them as
$$d\Phi_{Wg}=\frac{\hat{s}^{1-\varepsilon}}{16\pi^2} \frac{d\Omega^{1-2\varepsilon}}{(2\pi)^{1-2\varepsilon}}dxdv v^{-\varepsilon}(1-x-v)^{-\varepsilon}[2\pi\delta(x\hat{s}-m_{W}^{2})].\tag{4.11}$$
The factors in square brackets can now be recognised as the final-state phase space for $W$ production, at the reduced centre of mass energy squared $x\hat{s}$. The phase space is thus an integral over $x$ of the convolution of this reduced phase space with the dipole phase space defined by
$$d\phi(x,v,\hat{s})=\frac{\hat{s}^{1-\varepsilon}}{16\pi^2} \frac{d\Omega^{1-2\varepsilon}}{(2\pi)^{1-2\varepsilon}}dvv^{-\varepsilon}(1-x-v)^{-\varepsilon}\tag{4.12}$$
where the integral over $v$ ranges from $0$ to $(1-x)$. In this case, since only the partonic content in the leading order process is in the initial state, this corresponds to an initial emitter and initial spectator, anticipating the language of the Catani-Seymour approach that is used.

The dipole phase space derived above can be used to integrate the single dipole term of $(4.5)$. Restoring the correct overall dimensions with a factor of $\mu^{2\varepsilon}$,
$$\mu^{2\varepsilon}\int \mathcal{D}(\hat{t},x)d\phi(x,v,\hat{s})=\frac{\alpha_{s}C_{F}}{2\pi} \left( \frac{\mu ^2}{m_{W}^2} \right)^{\varepsilon}c_{\Gamma}x^{\varepsilon}\int_{0}^{1-x}dvv^{-\varepsilon}(1-x-v)^{-\varepsilon} \frac{1}{v} \left[ \frac{2}{1-x}-1-x \right]\tag{4.13}.$$
Here the convenient constant $c_{\Gamma}$ has been used. The $v$ integration can be evaluated by substituting the variable $v\to(1-x)y$ and using the result
$$\int_{0}^{1-x}v^{-\varepsilon}(1-x-v)^{-\varepsilon} \frac{1}{v}=-\frac{1}{\varepsilon} \frac{\Gamma^2(1-\varepsilon)}{\Gamma(1-2\varepsilon)}(1-x)^{-2\varepsilon}\tag{4.14}.$$
The result is a function of $x$ that is well defined as $\varepsilon\to 0$, except at the point $x=1$ where a non-zero value of $\varepsilon$ regulates the divergence. A convenient way of extracting this singularity is to employ the same $+$ distributions that were already used for the splitting functions. Applied to the case at hand, this allows the replacement
$$\frac{x^{\varepsilon}(1-\varepsilon)^{-2\varepsilon}}{1-x}\to\left[ \frac{x^{\varepsilon}(1-x)^{-2\varepsilon}}{1-x} \right]_{+}-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\left( 1+\frac{\varepsilon^2\pi^2}{3} \right)\delta(1-x)=\left[ \frac{{1+\varepsilon \log x-2\varepsilon \log(1-x)}}{1-x} \right]_{+}-\frac{1}{2\varepsilon}\left( 1+\frac{\varepsilon^2\pi^2}{3} \right)\delta(1-x)\tag{4.15}$$
where terms of order $\varepsilon^2$ or higher have been dropped. The remaining terms are non-singular in the limit that $x\to 1$ and so do not require the introduction of further $+$-distributions. Thus the integrand becomes
$$-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}x^{\varepsilon}(1-x)^{-2\varepsilon}\left( \frac{2}{1-x}-1-x \right)=-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left\{  \frac{\mathcal{P}_{qq}^{(1)}(x)}{C_{F}}-\frac{3}{2\varepsilon}\delta(1-x) - \varepsilon\left[ \frac{2}{1-x}\log \frac{(1-x)^{2}}{x} \right]_{+}-\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\left( 1+\frac{\varepsilon^2\pi^2}{3} \right)\delta(1-x)+\varepsilon(1+x)\log \frac{(1-x)^{2}}{x}  \right\} \\ = \left( \frac{1}{\varepsilon^2}+\frac{3}{2\varepsilon} +\frac{\pi^2}{3} \right)\delta(1-x)-\frac{1}{\varepsilon} \frac{\mathcal{P}_{qq}^{(1)}(x)}{C_{F}}+\left[ \frac{2}{1-x}\log \frac{(1-x)^2}{x} \right]_{+}-(1+x)\log \frac{(1-x)^2}{x}\tag{4.16}.$$
This ultimately yields, using the relation between Gamma functions,
$$\mu^{2\varepsilon}\int \mathcal{D}(\hat{t},x)d\phi(x,v,\hat{s})=\frac{\alpha_{s}C_{F}}{2\pi}\left( \frac{\mu^{2}}{m_{W}^{2}} \right) c_{\Gamma} \left[ \left( \frac{1}{\varepsilon^{2}}+\frac{3}{2\varepsilon}+\frac{\pi^{2}}{6} \right)\delta(1-x)+\left[ \frac{2}{1-x}\log \frac{(1-x)^{2}}{x}\right]_{+}-(1+x)\log \frac{(1-x)^{2}}{x} \right]-\frac{\alpha_{s}}{2\pi} \left( \frac{\mu^{2}}{m_{w}^2} \right)^{\varepsilon} c_{\Gamma} \frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathcal{P}_{qq}^{(1)}(x)\tag{4.17}.$$
There are two such contributions that take indentical forms originating from the $\mathcal{D}(\hat{t},x)$ and $\mathcal{D}(\hat{u},x)$ terms. The sum of the two is the form of the integrated dipole term represented by $\mathcal{I}^{(\mathcal{S})}$. Note that since the starting expression was defined in 4 dimensions, this calculation has implicitly been performed in the dimensional reduction scheme. Working in conventional dimensional regularisation would have introduced an extra term $-\varepsilon(1-x)$ in that equation that would eventually manifest itself as an additional $+(1-x)$ inside the square brackets.

With the explicit expression for this contribution at hand it is possible to see exactly how the factorisation of singularities into the parton distribution functions occurs. The relevant singular contribution is given by
$$\frac{\alpha_{s}}{2\pi} \left( \frac{\mu^{2}}{m_{w}^2} \right)^{\varepsilon} c_{\Gamma} \frac{-1}{\varepsilon}\mathcal{P}_{qq}^{(1)}(x)\tag{4.18},$$
since the remaining singularities proportional to $\delta(1-x)$ cancel with those obtained from the virtual diagrams. The NLO quark PDF $f_{q/h}(y)$ can then be defined in terms of the bare PDF $f_{q/h}^{(B)}(y)$, which does not exhibit any scaling violations, as follows
$$f_{q/h}(y)=f_{q/h}^{(B)}(y)+\frac{\alpha_{s}}{2\pi}\left( -\frac{c_{\Gamma}}{\varepsilon} \right)\int_{y}^{1} \frac{dx}{x} \mathcal{P}_{qq}^{(1)}(x)f_{q/h}^{(B)}\left( \frac{y}{x} \right)\tag{4.19}$$
in order to absorb this singularity. The factor of $1/x$ present in this equation can be traced back to $(4.7)$ while the dependence on $f_{q/h}(y/x)$ is a consequence of the fact that $x\hat{s}=m_{W}^{2}$. In addition to the pure $1/\varepsilon$ singularity, this definition also includes constant terms obtained by expanding out the universal factor $c_{\Gamma}$, 

Note that it is only pole term above that must be absorbed in order to arrive at a finite result while the constant terms are a matter of choice. The choice of additional, finite constant terms that are absorbed defines the factorisation scheme that is used for the PDFs. The definition above corresponds to the modified minimal subtraction, or the $\overline{\text{MS}}$-scheme, which is the preferred definition for all modern PDF sets. This scheme dependence must also account for the particular regularisation scheme used in the calculation. The redefinition corresponds to conventional dimensional regularisation. In the dimensional reduction scheme one must make the replacement,
$$\frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathcal{P}_{qq}^{(1)}(x)\to \frac{1}{\varepsilon}\mathcal{P}_{qq}^{(1)}(x)+C_{F}(1-x)-\frac{C_{F}}{2}\delta(1-x).\tag{4.20}$$
Hence, after this factorisation has been accounted for, the contribution of a single dipole term can be written $$\mu^{2\varepsilon}\int \mathcal{D}(\hat{t},x)d\phi(x,v,\hat{s})=\frac{\alpha_{s}C_{F}}{2\pi}\left( \frac{\mu^2}{m_{W}^{2}} \right)^{\varepsilon}c_{\Gamma}\left[ \left( \frac{1}{\varepsilon^2}+\frac{3}{2\varepsilon}+\frac{\pi^2}{6}-\frac{{1-a^{CDR}}}{2} \right)\delta(1-x)+1-x+\left( \frac{2}{1-x}\log (1-x) \right)_{+} - (1+x)\log \frac{(1-x)^2}{x} \right]\\=\frac{\alpha_{s}}{2\pi}\log\left( \frac{\mu^2}{m_{W}} \right)^2\mathcal{P}_{qq}^{(1)}(x)\tag{4.21}.$$
In this equation the regularisation scheme dependence has been captured by the constant $a^{CDR}$. Notice that once the dipole contributions are accounted for the dependence on $a^{CDR}$ in this equation exactly cancels out the virtual corrections. This result is very close to the one for the full real corrections, but differ by a single term which is given by the integral of the non-singular remainder
$$\mu^{2\varepsilon}\int \mathcal{R}(x)d\phi(x,v,\hat{s})=\frac{\alpha_{s}C_{F}}{2\pi}\left( \frac{\mu^2}{2m_{W}^{2}} \right)^{\varepsilon}c_{\Gamma}[-2(1-x)]\tag{4.22}.$$
