Hadronic collision events are broken down into different stages of scales related to momentum transfers, with the hard interaction as the starting point. As the hard scales are the largest, this allows fixed-order perturbation theory to be used. There, the relevant features of an event - the emergence and decay of heavy states such as gauge or Higgs or tops or hard QCD jets - can be included. This is done at leading order and small final state multiplicities by constructing the relevant Feynman diagrams, summing and squaring them by employing the completeness relations, convolution with the PDFs, and by integrating over the relevant phase space of the final state and over the momentum fractions of the initial-state partons with respect to the incoming hadrons. This integration is done using Monte Carlo methods as PDFs are numerical. As final state multiplicities increases, the number of Feynman diagrams exhibits a faster than factorial growth so numerical methods must be employed throughout.

Particles produced at this large scale will undergo some final state radiation, emitting Bremsstrahlung-quanta at lower scales. The partons will also experience initial state radiation due to the breakdown of the coherence of the Fock states related to the incident particles induced by the hard-interaction. These extra emissions can lead to logarithmic contributions, where the large values due to the $k_{\perp}$ integration partially compensate the small coupling. This leads to two regimes of parton emission:
- A regime of jet production, where $k_{\perp}\sim k_{\parallel}\sim \omega$ and emission probabilities scale like $w\sim \alpha_{s}(k_{\perp})\ll 1$; and
- A regime of jet evolution, where $k_{\perp}\ll k_{\parallel} \sim \omega$ and therefore emission probabilities scale like $w\sim \alpha_{s}(k_{\perp})\log^2k_{\perp}^2\gtrsim 1$.
This implies that in this jet evolution regime, typical fixed-order counting of coupling factors alone ceases to be sensible, and it becomes important to resum terms of the form $\alpha^{n}\log ^{2n}, \alpha^{n}\log ^{2n-1}$, and so on. This radiation is important for QCD particles because the strong coupling is larger than the electromagnetic one, and also gluons are coloured so emit secondary gluons. Such an enhanced radiation manifests itself in the emergence of jets, which turn out to be the relevant objects to discuss QCD final states. In programs known as event generators, the resummation of these logarithms and the resulting jet structure in typical QCD events is achieved numerically invoking parton showers. The logarithmically enhanced radiation pattern may have some sizeable impact on the structure of the events as it may change the kinematical distribution of particles in the final state. Note that the experimentally observable objects are hadrons rather than the quanta of QCD, quarks and gluons.

At low scales $\mathcal{O}(1\text{ GeV}^2)$, the perturbative description breaks down, and the impact of the transition to hadrons becomes important. To approach this, the main method uses Monte Carlo-event generators which, aiming to complete simulation of particle collisions including all facets, typically provide a phenomenological model that translates the partons into hadrons. These models usually contain a lot of parameters that need to be adjusted to existing data. Models use a qualitative description of how the phase transition proceeds and turns it into an algorithmic prescription. These models include independent fragmentation, the string model, and the cluster model. These are based off the idea of preconfinement. There are some analytical or semi-analytical descriptions that are employed, which are based on the analytical continuation of the strong coupling $\alpha_{s}$ into the non-perturbative regime and on employing the kinematics of perturbative parton splittings also in this infrared regime.

Hadrons that emerge in hadronisation processes are unstable and decay further, leading to an additional proliferation of particles in the final state. This is typically dealt with in event generators where, for lighter and well known hadrons have branching ratios from experimental results. For heavier channels, this ceases to be an option and additional decay channels must be invented and branching ratios fixed by considering involving phase space or flavour symmetry arguments. The kinematical distributions of the final state particles are often fixed by assuming isotropic decays, which may be a poor approximation. Therefore, in some cases matrix elements from effective theories or spin considerations only are invoked. For decays of hadrons with heavy quarks, often the final states are known, which involve a few final state particles only that can be taken from experiment. There, kinematical distributions are taken from suitably chosen matrix elements and often involve additional form factors, both obtained from heavy quark effective theory (HQET). For high multiplicity final states, often the decay is treated by going back to the parton level. In these cases, the parton-level matrix elements are supplemented with parton showers and hadronisation. 

Another complication when discussing collisions with hadron initial states comes from the fact that hadrons are extended objects, which are composed of a multitude of partons. One formalism is employing PDFs to account for the transition of the incident hadrons into the partons which then experience a hard scattering. However, this formalism does not account for the possibility that more than one parton pair coming from the two hadrons may interact. This effect, also known as double-parton scattering or multi-parton interactions, is beyond standard factorisation; in the absence of a first principles approach it thus can be modelled. Even the simplest models based on a factorised ansatz of multiplying hadronic cross-sections, and including a trivial symmetry factor, appears to agree with the data. This may suggest that probable correlation effects when going from one- to two-particle PDFs or non-trivial final state interactions are not dominant and can be treated as a higher order correction. Apart from such hard double or multiple interactions there are other, softer additional interactions when hadrons collide - some of them will just fall into the category of multiple parton interactions at lower scales, where no hard objects like jets with some tens of GeV in transverse momentum are produced. There is another contributor to the overall particle multiplicity, the remnants of the incident hadrons. After one or more partons have been extracted from them, the parton ensemble forming them typically cannot combine back into a single colourless object but instead must hadronise into a set of hadrons. However the two beam remnants appear connected in colour space which must be included into the reasoning. Additionally, although the partons are mostly moving parallel to the beam, i.e. to the hadron they form, there is no reason to assume that they do not have some transverse momentum of the order of $\Lambda_{QCD}$ up to some few GeV. his intrinsic transverse momentum of the beam remnant partons guarantees that the hadrons stemming from their hadronisation do not necessarily vanish along the beam pipe, but can instead reach the detector due to their finite transverse momentum.

Another important effect in hadron collisions is between multiple pairs of protons in the same bunch crossing, the pile-up. This is driven by the instantaneous luminosity of the collider experiment in question. Additionally, the size of the detector allows for the possibility of particles produced in more than one bunch crossing interacting with different parts of the detector at the same time, an effect known as temporal pile-up.
