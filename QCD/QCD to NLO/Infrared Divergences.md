Infrared divergences are more difficult to deal with than UV divergences. UV are only in the virtual correction so can be dealt with through regularisation or renormalisation, but IR live in both virtual and real corrections so these divergences must cancel.

The underlying idea in infrared subtraction is to isolate the divergent structures through suitable terms such that the master formula [(1.1)](Intro%20to%20NLO%20Processes) becomes
$$\sigma^{(NLO)}=\sum_{a,b}\int_{0}^{1}dx_{a}dx_{b}f_{a/h_{1}}(x_{a},\mu_{F})f_{b/h_{2}}(x_{b},\mu_{F})\int d\hat{\sigma}_{ab\to n}^{(NLO)}(\mu_{F},\mu_{R})=\int d\Phi_{\mathcal{B}}[\mathcal{B}_{n}(\Phi_{\mathcal{B}};\mu_{F},\mu_{R})+\mathcal{V}_{n}(\Phi_{\mathcal{B}};\mu_{F},\mu_{R})]+\int d\Phi_{\mathcal{R}}\mathcal{R}(\Phi_{\mathcal{R}};\mu_{F},\mu_{R})\\ =\int d\Phi_{\mathcal{B}}[\mathcal{B}_{n}(\Phi_{\mathcal{B}};\mu_{F},\mu_{R})+\mathcal{V}_{n}(\Phi_{\mathcal{B}};\mu_{F},\mu_{R})+\mathcal{I}_{n}^{(\mathcal{S})}(\Phi_{\mathcal{B}};\mu_{F},\mu_{R})]+\int d\Phi_{\mathcal{R}}[\mathcal{R}_{n}(\Phi_{\mathcal{R}};\mu_{F},\mu_{R})-\mathcal{S}_{n}(\Phi_{\mathcal{R}};\mu_{F},\mu_{R})]\tag{3.1},$$
where the real subtraction term $\mathcal{S}_{n}$ which lives in the $(n+1)$-particle phase space of the real correction, and the integration subtraction term $\mathcal{I}_{n}^{(\mathcal{S})}$, which lives in the $(n)$-particle phase space of the Born and virtual contributions, cancel each other in the integrals
$$0\equiv \int d\Phi_{\mathcal{B}} \mathcal{I}_{n}^{(\mathcal{S})}(\Phi_{\mathcal{B}};\mu_{F},\mu_{R})-\int d\Phi_{\mathcal{R}}\mathcal{S}_{n}(\Phi_{\mathcal{R}};\mu_{F},\mu_{R}).\tag{3.2}$$
The catch in this method is that due to the universal structure of infrared divergences in gauge theories it is possible to construct subtraction terms $\mathcal{S}$ in a process-independent way, guaranteeing that the difference $(\mathcal{R}-\text{subtraction})$ is finite in every single phase-space point in $\Phi_{\mathcal{R}}$. Even more these terms can be constructed as the product of some Born-level configurations times some individual terms, accounting for the emission of an additional particle in $D$ dimensions, yielding infrared divergences that manifest as terms proportional to $1/\varepsilon^2$ and $1/\varepsilon$.

The underlying idea can be shown in a toy model - assume that Born, virtual, and real emission matrix elements squared to $\mathcal{O}(\alpha)$ read
$$\mathcal{B}_{n}=\sum |\mathcal{M}^{(\mathcal{B})}_{n}|^2, \\ \mathcal{V}_{n}=\frac{V_{n}}{\varepsilon}=\sum |\mathcal{M}^{(\mathcal{B})}_{n`}\mathcal{M}^{*(\mathcal{V})}_{n}|, \\ \mathcal{R}_{n}=\frac{R_{n}(x)}{x}=\sum |\mathcal{M}_{n+1}^{(\mathcal{R})}(x)|^2,\tag{3.3}$$
where the Born and virtual contributions are constant in the toy model and the latter has already been regularised in $D$ dimensions and its ultraviolet divergences have been regularised and renormalised. The remaining infrared divergences then lead to the pole in $1/\varepsilon$, which has been made explicit in the virtual contribution. In the toy model $V_{n}$ is infrared finite and ultraviolet renormalised. The real emission part in the toy model depends on a one-dimensional phase-space parameter $x\in[0,1]$ and it diverges for $x\to 0$, producing a single pole $1/\varepsilon$, as again made explicit while the function $R_{n}(x)$ is regular in the full interval.
