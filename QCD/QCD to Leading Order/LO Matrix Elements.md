Going back to the master formula for [[Hadronic Cross-Sections]], equation $(7.1)$, there are some obstacles, one being the squared matrix element $|\mathcal{M}_{ab\to n}|^2$ must be evaluated. Since the number of diagrams increases very quickly, typically faster than a factorial, the traditional method of using diagrams, squaring amplitudes, and using completeness relations to arrive at a trace become impractical. Modern techniques focus on evaluating individual amplitudes as functions of their internal and external degrees of freedom, where every amplitude just becomes a complex number and in turn renders their summation and squaring a straightforward exercise.

The second obstacle is the complicated nature of the phase space in an integral in $(3n-2)$-dimensions. With complicated cuts, this high-dimensional integral becomes impossible to analytically solve, even if the PDFs were tractable in this way, As this is not the case, mainly numerical methods must be adopted over both the evaluation of the matrix elements and the phase space integration. For the phase space, only Monte Carlo integration techniques are viable, since for them the error estimate scales like $1/\sqrt{ N }$ for the number $N$ of integrand evaluations, independent of the number of dimensions.

Sampling this way to give a numerical estimate of the cross-section opens the possibility if sampling can be extended to other degrees of freedom like spin, colour, etc. In other words, for the matrix element evaluation, a choice between summation and sampling over quantum degrees of freedom must be made. This can be computationally expensive, however these factors can be reduced.

One of the first apparent problems in evaluating leading order scattering amplitudes is the summing over external polarisations through completeness relations, meaning that $\frac{N^2}{2}$ terms for a process with $N$ Feynman diagram. If we don't sum, each diagram is a complex number that depends solely on external momenta, colours and spins. This means for $n$ massless external particles with two helicity states each this would thus lead to $2^{n}$ complex numbers. The gain in computational effort comes from the fact that $N$ normally scales faster than factorially with $n$ and therefore the number of contributions becomes more like $\frac{N^2}{2}\leq (n!)^2$. This is the basic idea behind a set of methods known as the method of helicity amplitudes. In this method, any Feynman amplitude is translated into a complex number, dependent on external helicities and momenta. 

To save computing time, there are some tricks, for example the amplitude may vanish for certain helicity combinations. For instance, for massless fermions, a helicity flip in the interaction with a spin-1 particle is disallowed due to conservation of angular momentum, translating into the conservation of helicity along a fermion line and thus the vanishing of half the helicity combinations in the full process. Using such recycling recursively will reduce the number of complex multiplications like the ones in the spinor products and thus increase the efficiency of the calculation, especially when such common sub-amplitudes are identified in the construction of the helicity amplitudes and factored out from the beginning.

In order to identify and isolate such sub-amplitudes, it is important to realise that tensor structures in Dirac space in the numerators of propagators can be re-expressed through spinors as
$$\not\! p +m = \frac{1}{2}\sum_{\lambda}\left[ \left( 1+\frac{m}{\sqrt{ p^2 }} \right)u(p,\lambda)\bar{u}(p,\lambda)+\left( 1-\frac{m}{\sqrt{ p^2 }} \right)v(p,\lambda)\bar{v}(p,\lambda) \right].\tag{2.1}$$
A similar relation also holds true for propagator numerators for vector particles, with the added complication of gauge bosons. Typically to fix the gauge, a light-like gauge vector $q^{\mu}$ is introduced, essentially fixing the axis of an axial gauge and resulting in
$$-g_{\mu \nu}+ \frac{{q_{\mu}p_{\nu}+q_{\nu}p_{\mu}}}{pq}=\sum_{\lambda=\pm}\varepsilon_{\mu}(p,\lambda)\varepsilon ^{*}_{\nu}(p,\lambda)\tag{2.2}.$$
