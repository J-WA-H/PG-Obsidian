When evaluating the phase-space, traditional methods like the trapezoidal or Simpson methods don't converge fast enough for the large numbers of final state particles that we deal with, so Monte Carlo methods have to be used instead, which due to its statistical nature is guaranteed to have an uncertainty estimate that scales like $1/\sqrt{ N }$ with $N$ the number of phase-space points. The basic idea of Monte Carlo integration is to replace the integration of a function with sampling it:
$$I=\int_{V}d^{D}x\,f(x)\implies \langle I(f) \rangle _{x}=\frac{V}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f(\vec{x}_{i})=\langle f \rangle _{x}\tag{6.1}$$
Here $\vec{x}$ denotes a point in the $D$-dimensional finite phase space with volume $V$, and the $\vec{x}_{i}$ are uniformly randomly distributed in it. In Monte Carlo integration the exact value of the integral $I$ is estimated as an average over $N$ test points in the volume. The central limit theorem guarantees that with infinitely many calls $N\to \infty$ the estimator $\langle I \rangle\to I$. Monte Carlo integration aims to control this convergence through an error estimate $\langle E \rangle$ that will scale like $1/\sqrt{ N }$. A convenient error estimate is the variance, written in useful form as
$$\langle E(f) \rangle _{x}=\sqrt{ \frac{1}{N} \sum_{i=1}^{N}(f^2\left(\vec{x}_{i})\right)-\left( \frac{1}{N}\sum_{i=1}^{N}f(\vec{x}_{i}) \right)^2}=\sqrt{ \langle f^2 \rangle _{x}-\langle f \rangle ^2_{x} }\tag{6.2}.$$
Various methods can be used to accelerate error reduction, the most prominent ones being importance sampling and stratified sampling.

The underlying idea of importance sampling is to use a mapping of random numbers to function arguments $\vec{x}$, the four-momenta in the case of particle physics, which captures as far as possible the distribution given by the function, the squared matrix element for the process in question. In this method a probability density $g(\vec{x})$ for a vector of random numbers is constructed in such a way that $f(\vec{x})/g(\vec{x})$ is better behaved than $f(\vec{x})$ alone. Then the $D$-dimensional Monte Carlo integration of $f(\vec{x})$ over $D$ uniformly distributed random numbers $x_{i}$ becomes
$$\langle I(f) \rangle _{x}=\int d^Dx\,f(\vec{x})=\int d^Dx\,g(\vec{x}) \frac{f(\vec{x})}{g(\vec{x})}=\int d^D\rho  \frac{f(\vec{\rho})}{g(\vec{\rho})}=\langle I(f/g) \rangle_{\rho}=\left\langle \frac{f}{g} \right\rangle_{g}\tag{6.3},  $$
where the $\rho_{i}$ are distributed according to phase-space density $g(\vec{x})$. If $f$ and $g$ are sufficiently similar, their ratio $f/g$ will fluctuate less than $f$ alone, leading to a smaller variance and therefore an accelerated convergence. The limiting factor in this is obvious: a function $g$ must be found, which has to be inverted in order to generate the points $\rho$. Furthermore, to keep things simple, the $g(\vec{x})$ must be non-negative in $V$ and integrate to unity
$$\int_{V}d^Dx\,g(\vec{x})=1\tag{6.4}$$
which can be trivially achieved by normalising them to their integral over $V$.

Stratified sampling aims at improving the integration performance by binning the integration region $V$ and by distributing the phase-space points with a different density in the bins. The overall integral and the square of the overall variance are the sum and the quadratic sum of the integrals and squares of variance in the bins $b$:
$$\langle I(f) \rangle _{x}=\sum_{b}\langle I(f) \rangle _{x\in b} \\ \langle E(f) \rangle _{x}^2=\sum_{b}\langle E(f) \rangle ^{2}_{x\in b}\tag{6.5}.$$
The overall variance will be minimised by making the individual variances in each bin of equal size. This can be achieved by introducing a different a priori probability $a_{b}$ for picking a bin $b$ to generate a phase-space point in it, and by updating them regularly after a sufficiently large number of function calls. Bins with larger variance after such an optimisation step, with more fluctuations, will obtain a larger $a_{b}$ and therefore an increased number of points sampling the phase space in this bin. Ultimately, the best theoretical solution is to have a priori weights $a_{b}$ which behave like the variance or similar will accelerate the convergence.

Ignoring issues related to the sampling over the initial state parameters $x_{1},x_{2}$ or complementary $y_{cms},\hat{s}$, a logical first attempt at Monte Carlo phase-space integration for the final state consists of an isotropic phase-sampling. In such a sampling, for a given centre of mass energy squared $E_{cms}^2=P^2=\hat{s}$, the $n$ momenta $p_{i}^{\mu}$ populate the phase space homogeneously, but still respect overall four-momentum conservation and mass-shell conditions for the individual 4-momenta,
$$0=P^{\mu}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}^{\mu}=(E,\vec{0})-\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}^{\mu} \\ 0 = p_{i}^{2} -m_{i}^{2}=s_{i}-m^2\quad \forall \,i\in \{ 1,\dots,N \}\tag{6.6}.$$
These requirements are incorporated through $\delta$-functions in
$$d\Phi_{N}=\prod_{i=1}^{N}\left[  \frac{d^4p_{i}}{(2\pi)^{4}}(2\pi)\delta(p_{i}^2-m_{i}^{2})\Theta(E_{i}) \right] (2\pi)^{4}\delta^{4}\left( P^{n}-\sum_{i=1}^{n}p_{i}^{\mu} \right),\tag{6.7}$$
where $\Theta(E_{i})$ accounts for the projection on physical, positive energy solutions for the outgoing particles.

For massless particles, it is fairly straightforward to calculate the volume of the final stae phase space. As all ingredients in the $N$-particle phase space above are formulated in Lorentz-invariant quantities, in the following $P^{\mu}=(E_{cms},\vec{0})$ can be safely assumed. First an unconstrained phase-space volume $\tilde{\Phi}_{N}$ is introduced, based on unconstrained momenta $q_{i}$ not fulfilling overall momentum conservation like the constrained $p_{i}$:
$$\int d\tilde{\Phi}_{N}=\int \prod_{i=1}^{N}\left[ \frac{d^4q_{i}}{(2\pi)^4} (2\pi)\delta(q_{i}^{2})\Theta(q_{i}^{0})f(q_{i}^{0}) \right]=\left[ \frac{1}{(2\pi)^2} \int_{0}^{\infty} dx\,xf(x)\right]^{N}\xrightarrow{f(x)\to e^{ -x }} \frac{1}{(2\pi)^{2N}}. \tag{6.8}$$
Here $f(q_{i}^{0})$ denotes an arbitrary regulator function that keeps the overall volume finite. In a second step a transformation between unconstrained momenta $q_{i}^{\mu}$ and constrained momenta $p_{i}^{\mu}$ is found as a combination of a scaling operation parameterised by $x$ and a Lorentz boost given by $\vec{b}$,
$$p_{i}^{\mu}=xH_{\vec{b}}^{\mu}(q_{i})=x\begin{bmatrix}
\gamma q_{i}^{0}+\vec{b}\cdot \vec{q}_{i} \\
\vec{q}_{i}+\vec{b}q_{i}^{0} + \frac{(\vec{b}\cdot \vec{q}_{i})\vec{b}}{1+\gamma}
\end{bmatrix}\tag{6.9}.$$
Consequently, the inverse transformation is given by $q_{i}^{\mu}=\frac{1}{x}H_{-\vec{b}}^{\mu}(p_{i})$. With $M=\sqrt{ Q^{2} }$ the invariant mass of the unconstrained system, the boost parameters $\vec{b}$ and $\gamma$, and the scaling parameter $x$ read
$$\vec{b}=-\frac{\vec{Q}}{M},\quad \gamma=\frac{Q^{0}}{M}, \quad x=\frac{E}{M}\tag{6.10}.$$
Inserting these transformations into the unconstrained phase space and after a few manipulations, the two phase-space volumes are related with each other through
$$\int d\tilde{\Phi}_{N}=\int \frac{dxd^{3}\vec{b}}{(2\pi)^{4}} \frac{E^{4}}{x^{2N+1}\gamma}\left\{  (2\pi)^{4}\delta\left( E-\sum_{i=1}^{N}E_{i} \right)\delta^{(3)}\left( \sum_{i=1}^{N}\vec{p}_{i} \right)\prod _{i=1}^{N} \left[ \frac{d^4p_{i}(2\pi)\delta(p_{i}^{2})\Theta(E_{i})}{(2\pi)^4}f\left( \frac{1}{x}H_{-\vec{b}}^{0} (p_{i})\right) \right]  \right\}=\int \frac{dxd^{3}\vec{b}}{(2\pi)^4}\left\{  \frac{E^4}{x^{2N+1}\gamma}\prod_{i=1}^{N}\left[ f\left( \frac{1}{x}H^{0}_{-\vec{b}}(p_{i}) \right) \right]d\Phi_{N}  \right\}\tag{6.11}$$
with the choice made for the limiting function $f(x)$
$$\prod_{i=1}^{N}f\left( \frac{1}{x}H_{-\vec{b}}^{0}(p_{i}) \right)=\exp\left[ -\frac{\gamma E}{x} \right]\tag{6.12},$$
such that only the integration over $\vec{b}$ and $x$ remains, which results in
$$S_{N}=\int \frac{d^{3}\vec{b}}{(2\pi)^{3}}\int_{0}^{\infty} \frac{dx}{(2\pi)} \frac{E^4\exp\left( -\frac{\gamma E}{x} \right)}{\gamma x^{2N+1}}=\frac{E^{4-2N}}{(2\pi)^{3}} \frac{\Gamma\left( \frac{3}{2} \right)\Gamma(n-1)\Gamma(2n)}{\Gamma\left( n+\frac{1}{2} \right)}\tag{6.13}.$$
This fixes the unconstrained phase-space volume to
$$\int d\tilde{\Phi}_{N}=\frac{E^{2N-4}}{2(4\pi)^{2N-3}\Gamma(N)\Gamma(N-1)}\tag{6.14}.$$
To turn this into a Monte Carlo algorithm, it is sufficient to generate $N$ unconstrained momenta $q_{i}^{\mu}$, with isotropic angular distribution and an energy density given by $q_{i}^{0}\exp(-q_{i}^{0})$. Denoting with $\#$ a random number uniformly distributed in the interval $[0,1]$, the $q_{i}$ are obtained from
$$\phi_{i}=2\pi \cdot\#,\quad \cos \theta_{i}=1-2\cdot\#,\quad q_{i}^{0}=-\log(\#\cdot\#).\tag{6.15}$$
They are transformed into the constrained momenta $p_{i}^{\mu}$ through the transformations shown.
